We are living through the convergence of two worlds – the consumer devices collecting and relaying our data, and the regulated devices once sold only through clinicians – all powered by the AI that links them. The Device Files Summit gathers the people shaping how that future unfolds.

01

Consumer Tech

The phones, wearables, and sensors now collecting and relaying continuous data from everyday life – and the AI models turning that raw stream into something intelligent.

02

Regulated Medicine

The clinicians, regulators, and medical devices that once lived only inside the clinic – holding the standards of evidence, safety, and care that consumer signals must now rise to meet.

03

The Quantified Self

The new default: health measured not once a year, but every moment – and the questions of agency, equity, and access that follow.

04

AI in Health

The models and systems that can extend a stretched workforce – triaging, monitoring, and guiding – to help close care gaps and ease clinical shortages, so expertise reaches the people and places that go without.

Not a conference

A gathering, not an audience.

Most events are broadcast: a stage, a crowd, a queue for the microphone. The Device Files Summit is built on the opposite premise.

Everyone here participates. There are no passive seats. Each person is selected because of what they'll contribute to the room – whether that's leading a session, provoking a debate, or bringing a perspective no one else can.

We keep it small on purpose. One day, one shared table, and a group carefully composed to hold the most honest conversation of the year about where continuous health is headed.

On the table

Topics we may explore

A sample of the questions this room is built to move forward – illustrative, not exhaustive, and never a fixed agenda. The final sessions take shape with our founding participants.

Topic 01

Who owns the first conversation

The wearable now notices the change – HRV drift, a sleep pattern, a blood-pressure trend – before the patient or the doctor does. Whoever interprets that signal inherits the first conversation in healthcare, and the spending that follows it. What happens to the physician's role, and to the patient's trust?

Topic 02

One roof: monitoring, interpretation, routing, billing

A single company can now detect a signal, interpret it with AI, route you to a programme, and bill for it. Physicians are barred from self-referral; platforms are not. Is this the efficiency the system needs – or a patient-trust and antitrust question we haven't named?

Topic 03

When attention logic meets clinical judgment

Wearables were built to optimise engagement and monetise data. Now that same optimisation is steering clinical decisions – which specialist, which programme, which subscription. Who owns the most intimate record we've ever produced, and whose interest does the nudge serve?

Topic 04

The end of the wellness exemption

Regulators are lowering the barrier for some devices while closing the 'wellness' cover for medical-grade claims. Wellness versus medical device is now a strategic choice made at the whiteboard, not discovered in a warning letter. Where's the line, and who draws it?

Topic 05

Consumer tech is now MedTech

The moment a device detects, monitors, or manages, it crosses into medical territory – and strategy, evidence, and leadership all have to align around that. Is this the identity shift the whole field is trying to name?

Topic 06

Funding the convergence

AI and direct-to-consumer move at one speed; regulated medicine at another. This future needs early-stage investors fluent in both at once – comfortable with consumer velocity and clinical rigour in the same thesis. How do we educate investors to get comfortable investing at this convergence?

Topic 07

Who pays for continuous care

Monitoring only becomes care if someone funds the response to it. Reimbursement still rewards legacy volume, yet outcome-based pathways are beginning to open. Does continuous data lower total cost – or just add spend?

Topic 08

From clearance to standard of care

Breakthrough status is not a path to patients. Between coverage, EHR integration, and clinical workflow, the system loses most of what it approves. Where does it actually break?

Topic 09

Where the system places risk

As judgment shifts from clinician to algorithm to the person wearing the device, who is the backstop when it's wrong? If the physician is no longer the sole safety check, the system has to decide – deliberately – where accountability now sits.

Topic 10

AI as the accelerant

AI is what forces regulators to treat a wearable as more than a sensor – an interpretive engine that shapes care. Is AI the thing that finally makes wearables medicine, and can it extend a stretched clinical workforce without breaking trust?

A starting set, not a final one – founding participants help shape the questions and the sessions.
